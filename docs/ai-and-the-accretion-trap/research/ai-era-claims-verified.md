# AI-era claims and quotations in the seed report: verification (as of November 2026)

Scope: section 4 ("The Contemporary Battlefield", four AI cases) and section 5 ("Comparative Structural Taxonomy") of the machine-generated seed report in [thorwhalen/essays#30](https://github.com/thorwhalen/essays/issues/30). The early-internet sections are covered elsewhere. Method: each quotation was searched for in primary sources (papers, slides, court filings, transcripts, the speaker's own posts) and then in secondary reporting. A quotation that could not be located anywhere is reported as **NOT FOUND, likely fabricated**. Where a real statement exists near the fabricated one, its exact wording and source are given so the essay can substitute it.

**Facts to correct:**

- LeCun is no longer "Meta's Chief AI Scientist". He left Meta at the end of 2025 and founded AMI Labs, which raised $1.03B in March 2026 to build world models [9, 10].
- The seed's reference "Sutskever (2023) An Observation on Generalization and Next-Token Prediction, Stanford MLSys Seminar" is wrong. The talk is "An Observation on Generalization", Simons Institute (Berkeley), 14 August 2023, and it is about compression as a theory of unsupervised learning, not the detective analogy [13].
- Section 5 places Chollet among the "visionaries" and LeCun among "classical ML researchers". Chollet built ARC-AGI to show that LLMs lack fluid intelligence; LeCun is a founder of deep learning (connectionist), not a classical-ML skeptic. Both placements are wrong.
- The scaffolding diagram's "tree search (MCTS) + Process Reward Models" is not how the 2024–2025 reasoning models work. DeepSeek-R1 lists PRM and MCTS under "Unsuccessful Attempts"; its reasoning came from reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards with long chains of thought [47].
- "Inference-time scaling improves performance more cost-effectively than pre-training" is true only under specific conditions [46]; the headline example (o3 on ARC-AGI-1, 87.5%) cost about $4,560 per task at high compute [22].
- "Synthetic data filtered by validators prevents model collapse" overstates it. Shumailov et al. show collapse from *indiscriminate* recursive training; later work shows accumulating real data alongside synthetic data avoids it. Neither shows that verifiers alone prevent it [43, 44].
- The "Napster" framing of the copyright fight is out of date. Two US district courts held in June 2025 that training on lawfully acquired books is fair use; the liability came from pirated acquisition ($1.5B Bartz v. Anthropic settlement, final approval July 2026). The only appellate ruling (Third Circuit, Thomson Reuters v. Ross, 29 September 2026) rejected fair use, but for a non-generative system. The UK High Court held a diffusion model is not an "infringing copy". NYT v. OpenAI is still undecided on fair use [32–37].
- Kelly v. Arriba Soft concerned thumbnail images in search results, not "caching"; the operative opinion is the amended 2003 Ninth Circuit opinion [39].
- The abstraction box's "developer throughput multiplied 10x" has no source; the best randomized evidence on AI coding (METR) points the other way for 2025 [41, 42].
- Section 5's "Ultimate Outcome" column is a prediction presented as a fact. Label it as a prediction.

**Strongest counter-evidence the essay must face:**

1. METR's randomized trial (July 2025): 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks; AI use made them 19% slower, while they forecast a 24% speedup and afterwards believed they had been about 20% faster [41]. METR's February 2026 follow-up estimated an 18% speedup for returning developers (CI −38% to +9%) and 4% for new recruits (CI −15% to +9%), but METR calls this "only very weak evidence" because developers increasingly refused to work without AI [42].
2. The skeptics have already claimed the scaffolding as theirs. Marcus (July 2025): systems "that draw on code interpreter, and hence by definition are neurosymbolic, often outperform those that lack symbolic tools" [56]. The essay's "skeptics judge the bare model" framing must answer this.
3. Trust is low despite near-universal adoption: Stack Overflow 2025, 84% use or plan to use AI, 46% distrust its accuracy, 3% highly trust it, and 66% cite answers that are "almost right, but not quite" [51]; DORA 2025, 90% adoption and 30% little or no trust [53].
4. Reasoning models hallucinate more on some factual benchmarks (o3 33% and o4-mini 48% on PersonQA vs o1 16%) [48], and OpenAI argues that hallucination is driven by training and evaluation incentives, so it will not go away by itself [49].
5. Economic doubts: MIT NANDA's "95% of organizations are getting zero return" (disputed methodology), Altman's own "investors as a whole are overexcited", and Kedrosky's capex-bubble argument [27, 28, 58, 59].

**The best quotes for the essay's own side are real and recent:** DHH (August 2026), on what changed with the late-2025 models: "I don't know if Opus 4.5 was that much smarter than Opus 4 ... but its ability to instrument your computer, to use tools, to check its own work, to apply its intelligence in such a way that you could get real meaningful work out of it, was completely different" [20]. And Chollet (February 2026), warning that agentic coding inherits machine learning's failure modes: "overfitting to the spec, Clever Hans shortcuts that don't generalize outside the tests, data leakage, concept drift" [21].

## Case 1: "stochastic parrots" and world models

### Bender et al. (2021): VERIFIED, slightly truncated

The paper is Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and "Shmargaret Shmitchell" (a pseudonym of Margaret Mitchell), FAccT '21, pp. 610–623 [1]. The passage is on pp. 616–617, in section 6 ("Stochastic Parrots") [2]. The seed begins the quote at "An LM is a system for...", dropping "Contrary to how it may seem when we observe its output,". That is acceptable with an ellipsis, but the dropped clause matters to the argument: Bender's claim is about the gap between how outputs look and what produced them, which is close to the essay's own "judge the system, not the surface" point. Bender has continued the line in *The AI Con* with Alex Hanna (May 2025) [61].

### Gary Marcus, "Deep learning is good at perception, but terrible at reasoning...": NOT FOUND, likely fabricated (composite)

No source contains this passage or any of its distinctive sentences ("fun and amazing, but rarely reliable", "Manipulating symbols without understanding their semantic connection to the real world inevitably hits a wall"). Fragments resemble real statements: at the 2017 AI for Good summit Marcus said deep learning "is good at certain aspects of perception, particularly categorization, but perception is more than categorization" [4]; in a 2019 interview he said deep learning is "a very good tool for some things, but it's not good for abstraction, language, causal reasoning" [5]. The Nautilus essay "Deep Learning Is Hitting a Wall" (10 March 2022) is the canonical source [3]. Usable verbatim lines from it: deep learning is "fundamentally a technique for recognizing patterns", scaling is "perhaps already approaching a point of diminishing returns", and large language models are described (citing Bender and Gebru) as "repeating a lot, understanding little" [3]. When Sam Altman posted "there is no wall" in November 2024, Marcus replied that "multiple media reports from multiple companies are reporting diminishing returns, exactly as I warned in 2022" [24].

### Yann LeCun, "Auto-regressive generation is a dead end... It's not fixable. The error accumulation is exponential...": PARAPHRASE with a real core

The primary source is LeCun's slide deck for the NYU Philosophy of Deep Learning debate "Do large language models need sensory grounding for meaning and understanding?", dated 24 March 2023 [6]. Slide 9, titled "Unpopular Opinion about AR-LLMs", reads: "Auto-Regressive LLMs are doomed. They cannot be made factual, non-toxic, etc. They are not controllable. Probability e that any produced token takes us outside of the set of correct answers. Probability that answer of length n is correct: P(correct) = (1-e)^n. This diverges exponentially. It's not fixable." Two days later he posted on X that he had "claimed that Auto-Regressive LLMs are exponentially diverging diffusion processes" and that "one can mitigate the problem by making e smaller (through training) but one simply cannot eliminate the problem entirely" [7]. The seed's "dead end" wording belongs to later interviews, and its final sentence ("You cannot achieve true planning, hierarchical reasoning...") is not his.

Why it matters for the essay: the (1−e)^n argument assumes independent per-token errors and no correction. A loop that checks and repairs output (tests, a compiler, a verifier) breaks exactly that assumption. This rebuttal was made in April 2023 [8], so it is prior art, not the essay's discovery.

Where LeCun stands in 2025–2026: he left Meta at the end of 2025 and founded AMI Labs, which raised $1.03B at a $3.5B pre-money valuation (announced 9–10 March 2026) to build world models from video and sensor data [9]. A January 2026 New York Times piece was headlined with his warning that the industry is heading toward a "dead end" [10]. Secondary reports quote him calling LLMs a dead end for human-level intelligence while useful as tools; the exact wording of those interviews was not verified here.

### Ilya Sutskever, detective novel: COMPOSITE of two real sources, with fabricated embellishment

The first sentence is verbatim from the Dwarkesh Patel podcast of 27 March 2023: "Predicting the next token well means that you understand the underlying reality that led to the creation of that token." [12]. The detective analogy comes from the NVIDIA GTC fireside chat with Jensen Huang (March 2023, the day after GPT-4's release). From the transcript: "Say you read a detective novel. It's like a complicated plot, a storyline, different characters, lots of events, mysteries like clues, it's unclear. Then let's say that at the last page of the book, the detective has got all the clues, gathered all the people and saying, 'Okay, I'm going to reveal the identity of whoever committed the crime.' And that person's name is? - Predict that word." He then adds that predicting it "requires some amount of reasoning, a fair amount of reasoning" [11]. The transcript is auto-generated, so check it against the video before printing. The seed's "you have to have understood the entire narrative, the human psychology, and the physical constraints of the world" is not his.

### Andrej Karpathy, Software 1.0/2.0/3.0: definitions paraphrased; the "Ethernet" sentence NOT FOUND, likely fabricated

"Software 2.0" (Medium, November 2017) is real; it describes Software 1.0 as "written in languages such as Python, C++, etc." and Software 2.0 as "written in much more abstract, human unfriendly language, such as the weights of a neural network" [17]. "Software 3.0" is from his keynote "Software Is Changing (Again)" at YC's AI Startup School, San Francisco, 17 June 2025, where prompts in natural language are the programs and LLMs are "a new kind of computer" programmed in English [18]. The seed's definitions ("code written by neural network optimization based on an evaluation metric", "programs steered by prompts and agentic orchestration") are fair paraphrases but not his wording, so they should not sit inside quotation marks. The final sentence, "Dismissing the entire field because the bare underlying engine predicts tokens is like dismissing modern web applications because Ethernet drops packets", appears nowhere. It is the essay's own thesis, and attributing it to Karpathy is the single most damaging fabrication for this essay. Write it in the author's own voice.

DHH's arc is a better illustration for the essay than the fabricated quote: a skeptic who judged an autocomplete changed his mind when the harness (tool use, self-checking) arrived, and who still names the cost (architectural decay under accumulated agent changes). That cost is what the essay's title calls the accretion trap.

### François Chollet, "The problem isn't whether LLMs hallucinate...": NOT FOUND, likely fabricated; also misattributes his position

No source. The seed casts Chollet as a "visionary" of the scaffolded LLM camp, but he is best known for arguing that LLMs are pattern retrievers that lack fluid intelligence; ARC-AGI exists to test this [62]. Even his response to o3's ARC-AGI-1 result was guarded: it was "a surprising and important step-function increase in AI capabilities", but "Passing ARC-AGI does not equate to achieving AGI, and, as a matter of fact, I don't think o3 is AGI yet" [22]. His real statement closest to the essay's topic is an X post reported on 20 February 2026: "Sufficiently advanced agentic coding is essentially machine learning: the engineer sets up the optimization goal as well as some constraints on the search space (the spec and its tests), then an optimization process (coding agents) iterates until the goal is reached." He concludes that "all classic issues encountered in ML will soon become problems for agentic coding: overfitting to the spec, Clever Hans shortcuts that don't generalize outside the tests, data leakage, concept drift, etc." [21]. This supports the essay's scaffolding argument and warns about its cost at the same time.

For the point the fake quote was making, use Simon Willison, "Hallucinations in code are the least dangerous form of LLM mistakes" (2 March 2025): "Hallucinations in code are the least harmful hallucinations you can encounter from a model", because running the code exposes them; "Proving to yourself that the code works is your job" [23].

## Case 3: the data wall and scaling economics

### "Paul Kedrosky, writing with Gary Marcus in The Atlantic and on Substack": NOT FOUND, likely fabricated

No co-authored Kedrosky–Marcus piece exists in The Atlantic or on either Substack, and the quote cannot be found. Kedrosky's real positions are interesting for the essay because they cut both ways. With Eric Norlin (SK Ventures, March 2023) he argued that LLMs collapse the cost of software production and could pay down "society's technical debt", which is a pro-AI argument [27]. Since 2025 he has argued that AI data-centre capex is one of the largest capex bubbles in history and has compared it with railroad and telecom build-outs; the figures vary by method and should be taken from his own posts, not from summaries [28].

The real "wall" sources are Marcus's Nautilus essay (2022) [3] and the November 2024 reporting: The Information (9 November 2024) on OpenAI's "Orion" model showing a smaller jump than GPT-3 to GPT-4 [25], Bloomberg (13 November 2024) that OpenAI, Google and Anthropic were seeing diminishing returns [26], and Reuters (11–14 November 2024) quoting Sutskever that results from scaling up pre-training "have plateaued" [14].

### Sutskever, "The 2010s was the age of scaling pre-training... era of test-time compute... synthetic data verification": COMPOSITE

Real wording, Reuters (Krystal Hu and Anna Tong, November 2024): "The 2010s were the age of scaling, now we're back in the age of wonder and discovery once again. Everyone is looking for the next thing," and "Scaling the right thing matters more now than ever." [14]. NeurIPS 2024 test-of-time talk ("Sequence to sequence learning with neural networks: what a decade", Vancouver, 13 December 2024): "pre-training as we know it will unquestionably end" and "While compute is growing, the data is not growing, because we have but one internet" [15]. His "what comes next" slide named agents, synthetic data and inference-time compute as possibilities; secondary reports agree on this, but the seed's phrases "synthetic data verification" and "Compute during inference changes the scaling calculus completely" are not his. In November 2025 he told Dwarkesh Patel that 2012–2020 was the age of research, 2020–2025 the age of scaling, and that "we are back to the age of research again, just with big computers" [16].

### Marc Andreessen, "cost wall": NOT FOUND, likely fabricated

No source. In fact, on the a16z podcast of 5 November 2024, Andreessen said the leading models were "hitting the same ceiling on capabilities", with several now on par with GPT-3.5-era leaders, and that training data from the internet is limited ("there's just literally only so much") [29]. Citing Andreessen as an optimist against the cost wall gets his late-2024 position backwards.

### Data wall and model collapse: current evidence

- Epoch AI estimates the effective stock of quality public human text at about 300 trillion tokens (90% CI 100T–1,000T), and projects frontier training sets will match it between 2026 and 2032, with a median around 2028 (earlier if models are overtrained) [45]. No 2026 revision was found. Epoch itself lists ways around the limit: more learning per token, non-public data, other modalities and synthetic data.
- Shumailov et al., *Nature* 631 (July 2024): "indiscriminate use of model-generated content in training causes irreversible defects in the resulting models, in which tails of the original content distribution disappear" [43]. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) show the collapse is avoided when synthetic data accumulates alongside the real data instead of replacing it [44]. Together these support "curate and accumulate", which is weaker than the seed's "verifiers prevent collapse".
- Test-time compute: Snell et al. (August 2024) show that compute-optimal test-time scaling can beat a 14x larger model on some prompts, but the advantage shrinks on the hardest problems [46]. o3's ARC-AGI-1 result was 75.7% at about $26 per task and 87.5% at about $4,560 per task with 1,024 samples (ARC Prize's updated figures) [22]. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025; peer-reviewed in *Nature*, September 2025) reported that its reasoning came from reinforcement learning with rule-based rewards and listed PRMs and MCTS under "Unsuccessful Attempts" [47]; the RL stage reportedly cost about $294,000 on top of roughly $6M for the base model (secondary report) [47].
- GPT-5 (7 August 2025) fed the plateau debate: Marcus called it "overdue, overhyped and underwhelming" and an "incremental advance"; users complained about tone and regressions, while coverage also credited gains in coding, speed and cost [50].

## Case 4: copyright

### NYT complaint quote: first sentence VERIFIED; second sentence NOT FOUND, likely fabricated

The complaint (S.D.N.Y. No. 1:23-cv-11195, filed 27 December 2023, 69 pages) says in ¶2 on p. 2: "Through Microsoft's Bing Chat (recently rebranded as "Copilot") and OpenAI's ChatGPT, Defendants seek to free-ride on The Times's massive investment in its journalism by using it to build substitutive products without permission or payment." [31]. The sentence "A business model predicated on the mass misappropriation of intellectual property cannot stand" does not appear. The nearest real text is the section heading on p. 16, "A Business Model Based on Mass Copyright Infringement" [31].

### Andreessen, "We have seen this script play out with the phonograph...": NOT FOUND, likely fabricated

No source. The firm's real, on-record position is its comment to the US Copyright Office (October–November 2023): "Imposing the cost of actual or potential copyright liability on the creators of AI models will either kill or significantly hamper their development." The comment also says the industry invested "billions and billions of dollars" on the premise that such copying is fair use [30]. The VCR analogy was made in court by Microsoft's lawyers in the NYT case, not by Andreessen.

### The copyright picture as of November 2026

- **Precedents the seed cites.** *Sony v. Universal* (1984): time-shifting with a VCR is fair use, and a device "capable of substantial noninfringing uses" does not make its maker liable [38]. *Kelly v. Arriba Soft* (9th Cir., amended opinion 2003): thumbnail images in an image search engine are fair use [39]. *Authors Guild v. Google* (2d Cir. 2015): scanning books to provide full-text search and limited snippets is transformative fair use [40].
- **Bartz v. Anthropic** (N.D. Cal., Judge Alsup, 23 June 2025): training on lawfully purchased books was "exceedingly transformative" fair use; downloading millions of books from pirate libraries (LibGen, PiLiMi) to build a permanent library was not [32]. The $1.5B class settlement, about $3,000 per work for roughly 500,000 works, was preliminarily approved in September 2025 and given final approval in July 2026 [32]. Because it settled, the fair-use holding was never tested on appeal.
- **Kadrey v. Meta** (N.D. Cal., Judge Chhabria, 25 June 2025): summary judgment for Meta on fair use, but only because the plaintiffs offered no evidence of market harm. The judge wrote that "market dilution" could defeat fair use in a case with a better record [33].
- **Thomson Reuters v. Ross** (D. Del., Judge Bibas, February 2025: fair use rejected; Third Circuit affirmed 29 September 2026, the first federal appellate ruling on AI training). Ross copied 2,243 Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal search tool; the court called it "no more than an ordinary copyright case" and stressed that "ROSS's AI platform cannot generate original expression", distinguishing *Bartz* [34]. Ross has said it will seek Supreme Court review.
- **NYT v. Microsoft and OpenAI**: most claims survived the motion to dismiss (April 2025); the case is now part of *In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation* (MDL No. 3143, Judge Stein, S.D.N.Y.), with discovery orders covering tens of millions of ChatGPT logs. The Times amended its complaint in June 2026. Summary-judgment reply briefs on fair use were due 6 November 2026, so no fair-use ruling yet [35].
- **Getty Images v. Stability AI** (UK High Court, [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), 4 November 2025): Getty dropped its primary training claims (training happened outside the UK). On secondary infringement, the court held that model weights can be an "article" but Stable Diffusion is not an "infringing copy", because it does not store reproductions of the works. Getty won narrow trade-mark points on early versions. Getty was granted permission to appeal on 16 December 2025; no Court of Appeal ruling was found [36].
- **US Copyright Office**, *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 3: Generative AI Training* (pre-publication, 9 May 2025): some training uses will be fair use and some will not; commercial uses that compete with the originals in their markets go beyond established fair use. The Register of Copyrights, Shira Perlmutter, was dismissed the next day [37].

Net effect for the essay: the "inevitable legal collapse" did not come, and the Napster analogy failed, but for a different reason than the seed gives. Courts have so far separated the *training* (largely fair use for generative models) from *acquisition* (piracy is costly) and from *outputs and market substitution* (still open). The economic remedy has been settlements and licensing, not an injunction.

## Section 5 table: corrections

- "Primary Skeptics: ... classical ML researchers (LeCun, Marcus, Bender)". LeCun is a founder of deep learning; his objection is to autoregression, not to neural networks. Marcus is a cognitive scientist, Bender a computational linguist.
- "Primary Visionaries: ... (Karpathy, Sutskever, Hassabis, Chollet)". Remove Chollet (see Case 2). Hassabis is defensible, but nothing in section 4 supports it.
- "Engineering Mitigations: ... Process Reward Models (PRMs), Tree Search". As deployed, reasoning models rely mainly on RL with verifiable rewards; DeepSeek reported PRM and MCTS as unsuccessful [47]. Tool use, code execution, retrieval and test loops are the well-documented mitigations [54, 55].
- "Ultimate Outcome: The probabilistic model becomes the universal cognitive engine". Prediction, not outcome. Label it as such.

## Prior art and the other side

**Writing that already makes the essay's argument (credit it):**

- Berkeley AI Research, "The Shift from Models to Compound AI Systems" (Zaharia, Khattab et al., 18 February 2024): state-of-the-art results "are increasingly obtained by compound systems with multiple components, not just monolithic models" [54].
- Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (Schluntz and Zhang, 19 December 2024): successful agent systems use "simple, composable patterns" around the model [55].
- The April 2023 LessWrong rebuttal of LeCun's (1−e)^n argument, on the independence assumption [8].
- Simon Willison (March 2025) on running and testing code as the remedy for hallucination [23].
- DHH (August 2026) on the harness, not intelligence, as the change that converted him [20].
- Chollet (February 2026): agentic coding *is* optimization against a spec, which places the scaffolding argument inside ML and imports its failure modes [21].

**Writing that turns the argument around or argues against it:**

- Marcus, "How o3 and Grok 4 Accidentally Vindicated Neurosymbolic AI" (13 July 2025): "A lot of recent improvements are likely coming from improvements in using symbolic tools, rather than scaling" [56]. The skeptic says the scaffolding is the symbolic AI he asked for. The essay needs an answer: either the scaffolding is ordinary software engineering, not neurosymbolic AI, or the essay concedes that the skeptics' diagnosis was right and only their prognosis was wrong.
- Apple, "The Illusion of Thinking" (Shojaee et al., June 2025): reasoning models show "complete accuracy collapse beyond certain complexities" on controllable puzzles, and reduce reasoning effort as problems get harder [57]. Rebuttal: Lawsen (with Claude Opus as co-author), "The Illusion of the Illusion of Thinking" (arXiv 2506.09250), attributing much of the collapse to output-token limits and to unsolvable puzzle instances, and showing that models produce correct Tower of Hanoi solutions when asked for a program [57]. The rebuttal is itself an instance of the essay's argument: the bare model fails, and the model plus an interpreter succeeds.
